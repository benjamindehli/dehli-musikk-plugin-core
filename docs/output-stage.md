# The output stage

What each of the eight machines does, how the two measured ones were measured and fitted, and why the limiter looks ahead. The code is in `include/dm/dsp/OutputStage.h`, `Console.h` and `Cassette.h`.

The output stage is the last thing that happens to the signal. Whatever sets the level in front of it decides how hard it is driven. Its first job is to stop a mix pushed up from handing the host something louder than it asked for. It offers eight machines, which makes it part of the sound and not only a guard.

Every one of them stays within full scale. That is the purpose of the stage, and it is tested, because a safety clipper that could be driven past unity would not be a safety clipper. Each is tested at drives up to 400 times.

**Soft** is straight up to seven tenths and then bends into a tanh knee. It is gradual and symmetric, and adds odd harmonics as you drive it harder. It is the default. A test checks it bit for bit against its formula, written out longhand rather than taken from the code.

**Hard** cuts the signal flat at unity. Nothing happens below that, like a converter with no headroom left. It is abrupt and bright, with the same odd harmonics as Soft but all at once.

**Asymmetric** starts to bend one half of the wave before the other, at a half against nine tenths, the way a single-ended stage runs out of supply in one direction first. Soft and Hard treat both halves alike and give odd harmonics. Asymmetric gives even ones. Measured against Soft at the same drive, its second harmonic is a hundred times larger.

**Limiter** does not change the shape of the wave. It turns the level down as the signal approaches the ceiling and lets it back up afterwards, so you hear the level move, not the shape change. It is the only one of the eight a mastering engineer would recognise on its own.

**Fold** folds the wave back on itself past the threshold instead of flattening it. It is an effect that happens to stay within full scale, more than a protection. It makes far more harmonics than the others, and they move with the level, so its tone changes as you drive it where the others only get rougher. It is computed in closed form rather than in a loop, so a sample arriving at a hundred costs the same as one at a half.

**Console** is a console's mix bus, measured from a real chain at the settings it is used at: a passive transformer, a bus compressor and a tape stage, into the limiter with its ceiling at -0.1 dBFS. It changes over time as well as by level, because a compressor does. The chain was recorded one point at a time with a test signal from a DAW, at the transformer's output, the compressor's and the tape stage's, so each unit was fitted from what really went into it to what came out, at the level it sees in the chain. The gain staging between them is most of how hard each one works, and it is the chain's. The whole is held at unity for a quiet 1 kHz tone, so choosing it does not change how loud the mix is, and the chain's polarity, which the transformer inverts, is not kept.

The transformer saturates in the lows and nowhere else, because a core saturates on flux, the signal integrated over time. Its third harmonic is -35 dB at 30 Hz and -1 dBFS, -58 at 60 Hz and below -79 from 250 Hz up. It is modelled as the input through one pole at 15 Hz running into a knee, with what the core cannot carry lost from the output, which matches the measured harmonics within 2 to 4 dB and, unlike the polynomial fitted first, cannot turn back past the loudest level measured. Its passive high shelf, +4.25 dB above 8.1 kHz, is a first-order filter after it.

The compressor is 4:1, linked, with its sidechain high-passed at 120 Hz and an automatic release. It was fitted by running the transformer's pass through a model and matching the gain the compressor's pass shows in 5 ms windows, over level steps and bursts of 20 ms to 5 s, within 0.12 dB rms. The reduction is worked out from the level at once and then smoothed: up at a 20 ms attack, down at a 26 ms release, and a slow stage that charges over 1.3 s while the reduction is held and lets go over 3.5 s. So a short hit lets go in tens of milliseconds and a long one leaves a release of seconds, as the recording showed. Smoothing the level instead of the reduction could not catch a 20 ms hit at all, which the recording shows it does.

The tape stage has no time in it: its third harmonic is there at full strength within 20 ms of a loud tone and holds for five seconds. Once driven it holds near -34 dB however much harder it is pushed, which no smooth curve does. A share of the signal running into a hard limit while the rest passes does: just over the limit the clipped part grows fast, and well over it the clipped part is a steady fraction. So 7% of the signal, through a low shelf of +6.4 dB below 634 Hz since the lows reach the limit first, runs into a limit at -11 dBFS. The limit's corner is read as its average over each step, by its antiderivative, so it does not alias and adds no latency. A gentle low shelf, mostly the tape stage's bump, and the coupling capacitors' high-pass at 15 Hz come after, and with them the model's response follows the chain's within about 0.4 dB.

Put together and run over the whole test signal, the model follows the chain's level changes within about half a decibel at every frequency and level, and its third harmonic mostly within 1 to 6 dB at the loud levels. It runs only while it is chosen. Chosen part way through, it starts from rest and the output fades to it from what was running over 30 ms, which covers its filters settling. Only the compressor's slow release stage, seconds long, has not settled by then, and it only acts on something loud.

**Cassette** is a four-track cassette recorder at 9.5 cm/s with Dolby B on a normal tape, measured the way it is used, into the limiter with its ceiling at -0.1 dBFS. A test signal was recorded onto two of its tracks from a DAW at a normal level and played back, once with Dolby and once without, with a loopback of the interface to take its own response out. Each pass was lined up by a chirp at both ends, searched over three seconds, since a tape starts late and runs at its own speed: these ran 0.06 to 0.09% fast, wandering along the take. Reading a tone at exactly its own frequency failed above 8 kHz for the same reason, so every tone is read as the energy within about a percent of it. The interface takes 5.47 dB off on the way round, which the hiss is corrected for.

The record side saturates the lows first: at -3 dBFS the third harmonic is -18 dB at 30 Hz, -29 at 120 Hz and about -45 at 1 kHz, and a 315 Hz tone is clean to -8 dBFS. It is a low and a high shelf into a soft limit with the inverse shelves after it, within about 2 dB of the measured harmonics. Only what the limit takes away is averaged over each step by its antiderivative, since averaging the whole signal is a low pass of its own, 2 dB down at 10 kHz at 48 kHz, and the antiderivative is tabulated for that part alone, since tabulating the whole curve left an error the size of a -40 dBFS signal. The tape's own response is a head bump of +1.95 dB at 36 Hz and a roll-off to -10 dB at 18 kHz, within 0.16 dB. At 44.1 and 48 kHz the very top falls faster than the tape's, -12.6 dB at 16 kHz against -8, since the filters are bilinear and close to Nyquist there. At 96 kHz they follow it.

**Dolby B is two shelves that do not quite cancel.** The encoder lifts the quiet highs by up to 10 dB onto the tape and the decoder lowers them again, taking the hiss with them, each with a depth that gives way as the level in its high band rises. On this machine the decoder hears its input 4.85 dB quieter than the encoder sent it and their corners differ, so quiet highs come back 2 to 5 dB duller while loud ones barely change, which is how it sounds and is kept. Under a loud 315 Hz tone the machine still took 9 to 10 dB of hiss off above 4 kHz, which a side chain reading the shelf's own band cannot do. A sliding corner, which is how Dolby B is usually described, fitted worse here. What fits is a side chain that reads its level through a second high pass near 10 kHz. Each side chain averages the rectified signal rather than following its peaks, because the hiss's own peaks are four times its average and a peak follower let a third of the reduction go. Fitted to the highs at every level, the hiss removed in silence and under three tones, within 1.1 dB rms.

**The hiss follows the signal.** It is the tape's measured hiss without Dolby, white at -99.1 dB per hertz per channel above 400 Hz and rising below, independent on the two tracks, decoded by Dolby with everything else. It is at its full level once the output reaches -40 dBFS, less below that, and gone over 300 ms once nothing plays, so the deck is silent when nothing plays. Under a signal the tape adds noise of its own, and it follows the signal's highs: a 3150 Hz tone at -18 dBFS and a 315 Hz tone at -4 raised it by exactly the same, and those two are the same through a first order high pass at 1.6 kHz. So it is white noise at -79.2 dB per hertz against that band's level, letting go over 18 ms, which is how fast it fell away after a burst. With it the noise under every tone matches within about 1 dB without Dolby, and within 1.5 to 5 dB with it, highest under loud tones where the tone hides it.

**The transport is shared by both tracks.** The speed, read from a 3150 Hz tone as its instantaneous frequency, wanders by about 0.1% rms: steady flutter at 68 Hz and 40.8 Hz, noise in three bands from 0.2 to 20 Hz, and scrape flutter from 100 to 300 Hz. It moves a delay centred on a millisecond, which swung by up to 0.35 ms on the tape. That delay is part of the machine as an analogue filter's phase is, and is not reported as latency. The level wobbles by 0.25 dB rms at about 0.37 Hz as well. Crosstalk between the tracks measured -73 dB and the 50 and 100 Hz hum -85 to -92 dBFS, and both are left out.

The test signal was run through the C++ and through the same analysis as the recordings. The level follows the tape within about 1 dB from 30 Hz to 12 kHz at every level, the third harmonic within 2 to 3 dB at the loud levels, the flutter at 0.09% against 0.11% with the same rates, and the hiss coming back after a burst within about 1.5 dB at every step. Like Console it runs only while it is chosen, costing about 0.9% of one core at 48 kHz, and chosen part way through it starts from rest with the output fading to it over 30 ms, which covers the delay starting empty and the filters settling. Dolby's depths and the wobble are worked out every eight samples, since each is a power and none moves in less than a millisecond.

**Print** is the console's chain printed to the cassette recorder, in the order they were patched: Console, a limiter at -0.1 dBFS, then Cassette, into the limiter at the end. The outboard chain was measured at the level of the DAW and the deck at a normal recording level, which are the same reference, so the two models go together as they are with nothing in between but the limiter the outboard chain ends in. That limiter has no lookahead: its gain falls at once to what the sample needs and recovers over 100 ms, as the output stage's own does with the lookahead off. From moderate levels it has the console's steady third harmonic, -39 dB at 1 kHz and -9 dBFS where the deck alone is still at -60, and at the top the compressor turns the loudest parts down before the deck, so a full-scale 1 kHz tone saturates the tape a little less than Cassette alone, -36 dB against -31, while 60 Hz still saturates it first, at -17. Its tape hiss sits 6 dB under the deck's on its own, set by ear, since running the outboard chain first is what puts the music onto the tape at a better level against the hiss. Recording 6 dB hotter onto the model of the deck would have lowered the hiss as much, but the deck was measured at a normal recording level where full scale already reaches its limit, and 6 dB more took loud bass to a third harmonic of -10 dB. So the floor is lowered and the rest of the deck is left as measured. Print has its own console and deck, so switching between it and Console or Cassette fades with both running, and it costs about what the two do together, only while it is chosen.

**The limiter looks ahead, and that is why the stage has latency.** Without lookahead, the gain only starts to move once the peak has arrived, so the hard clip behind the limiter does the real work. On a mix driven hard, two seconds of audio reach 1.294 and have 1741 samples cut off. In that case the limiter is in effect a clipper.

The lookahead is two milliseconds, and the whole gain path fits inside it. A causal box filter of B samples delays its output by (B-1)/2, so a detector window of W followed by two boxes costs (W-1)+(B-1). With W = B = half the delay, that is two samples less than the budget. One box would turn the gain step into a straight ramp with a corner at each end. The second box rounds off those corners, so the gain arrives in time without being heard. There is no attack coefficient. How fast the gain can fall is set by how far ahead it can see.

| Lookahead | Peak before the backstop | Samples clipped | Gain movement |
| --------- | ------------------------ | --------------- | ------------- |
| none      | 1.294                    | 1741            | 0.0041        |
| 1 ms      | 0.980                    | 0               | 0.0040        |
| 2 ms      | 0.981                    | 0               | 0.0020        |
| 5 ms      | 0.983                    | 0               | 0.0007        |

Every size removes the clipping, so the choice is between how gently the gain moves and how much latency it costs. Five milliseconds would give a curve two and a half times gentler still, a difference too small to hear, for three more milliseconds. At two, an abrupt onset at twice full scale holds at 0.98, and the third harmonic the limiter adds falls from 0.00259 to 0.00012.

**Every type pays that latency, and so does the stage when it is off.** A stage that reported a different figure for each type would make the host re-plan its graph every time the type changed. With the lookahead on, the stage is late by two milliseconds, 96 samples at 48 kHz. With it off, it has no latency.

**Without the lookahead the stage is rougher, not broken.** Only the Limiter, Console, Cassette and Print use the window. With the lookahead off, the detector sees a single sample rather than a window. The gain it applies is then exact and immediate, and the ceiling still holds without the clip behind it having anything to do. What is lost is the smoothing, and a gain that steps is itself a distortion. The third harmonic goes from 0.00012 to 0.00185, about fifteen times higher. That makes a rougher limiter, but not the one with no lookahead at all, which overshoots to 1.294 and leaves the clip to catch it.

A plugin that lets the lookahead be switched should report the change in latency from the message thread, for example by polling on a timer, not from a parameter listener. A listener runs on whichever thread moved the parameter, and a host automating it would move it from the audio thread, which is the wrong thread for telling a host its graph has changed. Hosts handle a latency change during playback differently. Most re-plan, and some only notice when playback stops or on reload, so it is a setting to choose once and leave, not one to automate. The hard clip stays behind the limiter, with nothing to do, because the stage has to stay within full scale even at a rate low enough that the window is only a couple of samples.
